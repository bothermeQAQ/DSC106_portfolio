## NYC Airbnb Price Prediction / Price Intelligence

### Core idea

The cover should communicate a serious machine learning pricing system for NYC short-term rental listings. It should feel like a product-facing intelligence layer built from listings, neighborhood signals, text features, geospatial context, and model performance, not a generic Kaggle trophy graphic or dark AI poster.

### Visual metaphor

Use a city-pricing signal map: a stylized NYC borough grid where listing cards, price bands, prediction intervals, and model-confidence traces sit over neighborhood geometry. The central metaphor is a rental market being transformed into a readable pricing surface, with model outputs explaining why one area or listing is more expensive than another.

### Mood and tone

Analytical, urban, practical, and product-minded. It should feel like a polished data product for pricing strategy: confident but not flashy, technical but legible, closer to an urban intelligence dashboard than a competition poster.

### Color direction

- Deep urban navy and ink blue for the city-grid base.
- Warm apartment-window amber for rental/listing signals.
- Muted data green for model confidence and price-improvement cues.
- Soft concrete gray and off-white for map surfaces and interface panels.
- Small red/orange accents only for outlier prices or high-error zones.

### Composition

Dashboard-inspired with a map as the main structure. The focal point should be a simplified NYC map/grid with price prediction overlays, supported by a small prediction curve, a feature-importance strip, and a few listing-card fragments. The cover can be semi-technical and product-like, but it should not depend on tiny unreadable interface text.

### Key visual elements

- Stylized NYC borough or street-grid geometry.
- Listing-card fragments with price bands or nightly-rate marks.
- Prediction curve or confidence interval.
- Geospatial dots clustered by neighborhood.
- Feature-signal chips for text, amenities, reviews, and location.
- A subtle model-performance marker such as MAE or leaderboard-style metric.
- Neighborhood/zipcode encoding represented as layered regions.
- Stacked model ensemble motif, such as three translucent model layers merging into one prediction.

### Avoid

- No official Airbnb logo or brand marks.
- No generic AI brain, robot, or glowing neural-network cliche.
- No trophy/medal-first Kaggle poster.
- No exact screenshots of Airbnb, Kaggle, or real listing UI.
- No unreadable dense dashboard text.
- No one-size-fits-all monochrome poster template.
- No overly luxurious real-estate photography that hides the ML/data story.

### Image prompt draft

Create a 4:3 cover image for "NYC Airbnb Price Intelligence", a machine learning project that predicts NYC Airbnb nightly prices. Show a stylized NYC borough and street-grid map transformed into a pricing intelligence dashboard: neighborhood clusters, listing-card fragments, price bands, geospatial dots, a prediction curve with confidence interval, feature-signal chips for location, amenities, reviews, and text features, and three model layers merging into one final prediction. The mood should be urban, analytical, product-ready, and practical. Use deep urban navy, concrete gray, warm apartment-window amber, muted model green, and off-white interface surfaces. Make it feel like a real pricing data product, not a generic AI poster. Minimal readable text is allowed: "NYC Airbnb Price Intelligence", "Pricing / Geospatial / NLP", "Predict - Explain - Compare". Avoid official Airbnb branding, exact platform UI, Kaggle trophies, AI brains, and tiny unreadable labels.

### Alternative style option

Use an editorial "rental market atlas" direction: a clean illustrated NYC map spread with annotated price contours, listing pins, and a model notebook margin. This would feel more like a data journalism feature than a SaaS dashboard while still emphasizing geospatial pricing and model explainability.

## Warming Unevenly — DSC106 Project 3

### Core idea

The cover should communicate that warming is not uniform across the planet. It should show regional differences, climate-scenario comparison, polar amplification, and trend exploration in a way that feels like climate data visualization rather than generic Earth imagery.

### Visual metaphor

Use split-scenario climate layers: the world is divided into regions with different heat-anomaly intensities, while an Arctic/polar cap region visibly accelerates faster than lower latitudes. The metaphor is a climate map under multiple futures, where users can compare pathways and inspect how each region diverges from the baseline.

### Mood and tone

Scientific, urgent, comparative, and exploratory. The tone should be sober but visually engaging: more climate research interface than disaster poster. It should suggest interactive analysis, not just a static warning image.

### Color direction

- Cool ocean blue and pale cyan for baseline/cooler anomalies.
- Thermal orange, coral, and deep red for stronger warming.
- Violet or magenta edge accents for extreme polar anomaly regions.
- Neutral parchment or light gray map labels for scientific readability.
- Dark slate only as a support color for axes, trend lines, and scenario panels.

### Composition

Map-based and data-viz-forward. The focal point should be a world/regional anomaly map with the Arctic visually separated or emphasized. Add side-by-side scenario strips, a year slider cue, and a linked trend chart/ranking panel. The cover should look intentionally designed, but not like a screenshot of the live project.

### Key visual elements

- Regional climate anomaly map with uneven heat intensity.
- Arctic or polar cap highlight showing amplification.
- Scenario comparison bands or small multiples.
- Baseline marker for 1995-2014.
- Year slider or temporal sweep.
- Trend line showing anomaly growth over time.
- Ranking bars or regional comparison marks.
- Tooltip/detail-panel motif for interactive exploration.

### Avoid

- No generic photorealistic Earth wallpaper.
- No burning planet cliche.
- No disaster imagery such as floods, fires, or cracked ground as the main concept.
- No excessive red/orange wash that erases regional contrast.
- No official climate-agency marks unless explicitly licensed.
- No tiny scientific labels that must be read to understand the image.
- No same-template dark dashboard treatment used for unrelated projects.

### Image prompt draft

Create a 4:3 cover image for "Warming Unevenly", an interactive climate data visualization about regional warming differences under CMIP6 scenarios. Show a designed climate anomaly map with regions warming at visibly different rates, an emphasized Arctic/polar region showing polar amplification, side-by-side scenario comparison bands, a subtle year-slider cue, a trend line, and a small regional ranking panel. The image should feel like a climate-science visualization interface, not a generic Earth wallpaper. Use cool ocean blues and pale cyan for baseline areas, thermal orange/coral/deep red for stronger anomalies, and a restrained violet accent for extreme polar amplification. Minimal readable text is allowed: "Warming Unevenly", "CMIP6 Scenarios", "Scenario - Compare - Explore". Avoid burning planet imagery, disaster-photo aesthetics, official institutional marks, and unreadable tiny labels.

### Alternative style option

Use a scientific small-multiples direction: three or four clean map panels arranged like a climate report figure, each showing a different scenario/year combination, with a bold Arctic inset and an anomaly legend. This would be more research-paper-inspired and less product-interface-driven.

## Bikewatching

### Core idea

The cover should communicate urban bike movement through Boston/Cambridge as a living mobility network. It should feel like a designed identity for a geospatial flow visualization, not simply a prettier screenshot of the existing Mapbox/D3 interface.

### Visual metaphor

Use bike-flow trails through an urban map: station nodes pulse on a simplified Boston/Cambridge street grid while directional flow lines trace commute paths and time-of-day movement. The metaphor is a city breathing through bike-share traffic.

### Mood and tone

Energetic, civic, observational, and map-driven. It can be more active and kinetic than the other covers, with a transit-system feel and visible movement. It should feel local and urban rather than abstractly technical.

### Color direction

- Charles River blue and harbor teal for water/route context.
- Bike-lane green for active station/flow signals.
- Warm commuter yellow for time-of-day movement pulses.
- Asphalt gray and map-paper off-white for street-grid structure.
- Small coral/red accents only for directional hotspots or high-traffic stations.

### Composition

Map-based with motion as the focal point. Use a simplified street-grid/river silhouette as the base, then layer station dots and directional arcs. A subtle time filter or clock/range control can sit as a secondary visual cue. The image can be illustrative and technical, closer to a mobility-map poster than a product dashboard.

### Key visual elements

- Boston/Cambridge-inspired street-grid texture.
- River shape or waterfront divide as geographic context.
- Bike station dots sized by traffic volume.
- Directional flow lines or arcs between clusters.
- Time-of-day sweep or filter control motif.
- Density hotspots around commute corridors.
- Small D3/Mapbox-style chart marks, without copying real UI.
- A sense of movement from one side of the city to another.

### Avoid

- No official BlueBike, Mapbox, or city-agency logos.
- No raw screenshot replacement.
- No generic bicycle illustration with no data layer.
- No cluttered transit map with too many colors.
- No exact real map tiles or copyrighted cartography.
- No uniform dark poster style copied from other project covers.
- No tiny station labels that become visual noise.

### Image prompt draft

Create a 4:3 cover image for "Bikewatching", an urban mobility data visualization of Boston/Cambridge bike-share traffic built with Mapbox and D3. Show a simplified Boston/Cambridge street-grid and river silhouette, bike station dots sized by activity, directional flow lines between station clusters, a subtle time-of-day sweep, and density hotspots that suggest commuting movement. The image should feel like a designed mobility-data identity, not a raw app screenshot. Use Charles River blue, harbor teal, bike-lane green, warm commuter yellow, asphalt gray, and off-white map neutrals. Minimal readable text is allowed: "Bikewatching", "Urban Mobility / Mapbox / D3", "Flow - Map - Explore". Avoid official bike-share logos, Mapbox branding, exact map tiles, generic bicycle poster art, and overly cluttered transit-map colors.

### Alternative style option

Use a risograph-inspired urban mobility print: simplified street blocks, river shape, station dots, and flowing route trails in a limited blue/green/yellow palette. This would make the cover feel more graphic and local, while still clearly representing geospatial movement data.
